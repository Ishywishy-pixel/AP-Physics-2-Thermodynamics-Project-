System and energy transfers

  • System: the 1 mol of ideal gas in the cylinder.
  • Surroundings: the piston (you) and the water bath.
  • Work: into the gas (W > 0) because the piston pushes inward.
  • Heat: out of the gas into the bath (Q < 0), the same size as W.
  • ΔU = 0, because U of an ideal gas depends only on T.

Assumptions

  • Ideal gas: point molecules, no forces between them.
  • Quasi-static: slow enough that the gas is in equilibrium at every step.
  • The bath is big enough that its temperature never changes.
  • Closed cylinder (n = 1 mol) and a frictionless piston.

Limitations

  • Tiny volumes / high pressures: real gases stop acting ideal and can liquefy, so V stops at 0.006 m³.
  • Heat leaves through the wall at a finite rate (time constant 0.03 s, an estimate). A fast push leaves no time for it, so T rises. Watch the thermocouple meter.
  • The moving dots are a 2D picture. The numbers come from the equations, not from counting dots.

Real-world applications

Slow compression with heat flowing out shows up in more places than you'd think.

1Bike and hand pumps

Pump slowly and the barrel stays cool. Pump hard and fast and it gets noticeably warm.

Link to T2Slow pumping gives heat time to leave, so it's close to isothermal. Fast pumping is closer to the insulated what-if: the work stays in the air and T rises.

2Filling scuba tanks

Air is squeezed to around 200 times atmospheric pressure, and the tank heats up while it fills. Dive shops fill slowly, and some put the tank in a water bath, just like this lab.

Link to T2The bath carries heat away so the air stays near room temperature. A tank filled hot loses pressure as it cools, because P is proportional to T at a fixed volume.

3Multi-stage air compressors

Industrial compressors squeeze air in stages and cool it between each one with intercoolers.

Link to T2The isothermal curve sits lowest on the P–V graph, so it has the least area and takes the least work. Halving the volume at 300 K takes 1,729 J isothermally but 2,198 J insulated, so the isothermal path saves about 21%.

4Stirling engines

One step of the ideal Stirling cycle is exactly this process: gas is compressed while heat flows out to a cold reservoir. Stirling engines show up in cryocoolers, solar dish generators and some submarines.

Link to T2The engine uses the same energy rule as this lab. The work in during compression leaves as heat (Q = −W), and the cycle later turns heat back into work at a higher temperature.

5Compressed-air energy storage

Spare electricity from wind or solar can be stored by compressing air. Some designs spray water mist into the air while compressing it to pull the heat out as they go.

Link to T2Keeping the air close to isothermal cuts the work needed. The heat that leaves can be stored and reused, so less energy is wasted.
